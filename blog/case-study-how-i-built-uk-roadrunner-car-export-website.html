How I Built UK RoadRunner: Vehicle Discovery, Export Guidance & Enquiries

Samuel Kidemi Kisaga Founder & Principal Web Architect
• November 9, 2026 • 6 min read • Live Site: ukroadrunner.co.uk →
UK RoadRunner Limited · Automotive & international vehicle exports · Website by Samuel Kidemi

Buying a vehicle overseas starts with questions: which car fits my needs, what happens after I choose it, and who can help me through the import process? When I built UK RoadRunner’s website, I organised the experience around those decisions.

This case study walks through the customer-facing design: a searchable showroom, practical export information and routes into a conversation with the team. The previews are real desktop captures of the live website taken on November 9, 2026.

Project at a glance

  • Client: UK RoadRunner Limited.
  • Sector: vehicle sourcing and international automotive logistics.
  • My work: website design and development.
  • Design focus: help buyers discover vehicles, understand the export journey and make a relevant enquiry.
  • Explore: Visit UK RoadRunner.

1. Give the first screen a useful job

I brought vehicle discovery into the homepage instead of making buyers search through company information first. The search offers make, model, price, mileage and year controls, alongside a separate finance search tab. The visual direction pairs a deep purple identity with orange actions and prominent vehicle imagery.

The broader design principle is simple: a strong first screen should explain the offer and let visitors do something useful. A buyer who already knows their preferred vehicle can begin searching; someone still researching can explore the services and process.

2. Make inventory easier to narrow down

The inventory page combines a filter sidebar, brand shortcuts, sorting and vehicle cards. This gives visitors several ways to reduce a broad catalogue to a more useful shortlist.

I used a repeatable card structure so the photograph, vehicle identity and key information occupy predictable places. Consistency helps comparison: visitors can focus on the differences between cars instead of relearning the layout for each listing. Stock and prices are live business information and will change after these screenshots.

3. Connect the showroom to the export journey

Vehicle choice is only one part of this business. I separated the navigation into areas for finding a car, services, destinations and the import process. That structure gives different questions a clear home without forcing the entire service catalogue into the main page.

The Kenya shipping page brings together shipping options, import preparation and a quote request. Destination-specific content helps buyers reach information relevant to their route, rather than treating every overseas enquiry as the same transaction.

4. Give research a clear next step

The site includes an import-duty estimate tool and a route to request a detailed cost breakdown. The calculator’s role in the interface is to support an initial budgeting conversation; its own disclaimer distinguishes an estimate from a final assessment.

I kept the contact action visible in the navigation. A useful commercial website should let someone move from browsing to a specific question without losing context. The design supports that handoff rather than requiring every visitor to follow an identical path.

5. What this project demonstrates

  • Discovery: give catalogue visitors practical ways to narrow their options.
  • Clarity: separate product information from service and destination guidance.
  • Consistency: repeat navigation, card patterns and action styles.
  • Enquiries: connect research content to a relevant conversation.

The finished work is a vehicle export website that brings these journeys together. This walkthrough focuses on the interface and content decisions visible in the live project; it does not claim a measured increase in sales or enquiries.
