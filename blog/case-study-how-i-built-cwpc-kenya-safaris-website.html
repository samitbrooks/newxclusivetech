Safari Websites & Direct Enquiries

How I Built CWPC Kenya Safaris: From Safari Discovery to Direct Enquiries

Samuel Kidemi Kisaga Founder & Principal Web Architect
• November 7, 2026 • 5 min read • Live Site: cwpckenyasafaris.com →
CWPC Kenya Safaris · Safari & experiential travel · Elite Designs Kenya project

A safari website has to do more than show beautiful wildlife. It needs to help a traveller understand the route, compare options and feel ready to ask a useful question. That is the design problem at the centre of CWPC Kenya Safaris.

In this walkthrough, I explain the project’s approach to discovery, trip presentation and direct enquiries. CWPC is an Elite Designs Kenya project, featured here in the Xclusive Tech portfolio. The screenshots show how the website brings those decisions together.

Project at a glance

  • Business: CWPC Kenya Safaris, an East African safari and experiential travel operator.
  • Design focus: destination discovery, structured safari information and a clear enquiry journey.
  • Experience: wildlife safaris, gorilla trekking, Kilimanjaro climbs and beach holidays.
  • Explore: Visit the CWPC website or read the Elite Designs project summary.

1. Start with the traveller’s decisions

The design follows the sequence a guest takes: where to go, which kind of trip to choose, what the journey includes and how to speak with the operator. A single undifferentiated list of tours would make those decisions harder. The CWPC navigation provides separate entry points for destinations, safari types, hiking and Zanzibar.

This lets a guest begin with a country, an experience such as a family safari, or a specific interest such as climbing Kilimanjaro. The benefit is practical: different travellers can reach relevant information without first having to understand the operator’s entire catalogue.

2. Make the first screen explain the offer

The homepage combines wildlife photography with a direct message: “Compare, Choose & Customize.” The supporting text names the types of trips and the countries served. I see this pairing as essential: the image creates interest, while the copy tells a prospective guest whether the business is relevant to their plans.

Two actions serve different levels of readiness. “View All Tours” suits someone still researching; “Start Planning Your Safari” gives someone with an idea a route into a conversation. The dark image treatment also gives the white headline and outlined buttons a clear visual backdrop.

3. Give each safari a useful information structure

The live homepage’s featured packages show duration, destination, trip type, a short description, a starting price and a link to the tour. These fields help visitors distinguish a short fly-in trip from a longer cross-border safari before opening the full itinerary.

The project also brings together day-by-day itineraries, lodge classifications and seasonal pricing. The design principle is consistency: comparable information should appear in comparable places. A guest should not have to hunt through several paragraphs to understand what changes between two options.

Starting prices are an introduction to the offer, not a substitute for the final quote. Dates, accommodation choices and party size still need to be discussed with the operator. The website’s role is to prepare that discussion with clearer information.

4. Keep the next step close to the content

The homepage includes an “Inquire Now” navigation action, a safari-planning action and a visible WhatsApp contact. I treat these as complementary paths: some guests want to browse more pages, while others prefer to ask a person about dates and suitability.

For a tailor-made safari, a direct enquiry is a useful outcome in its own right. It gives the operator an opportunity to understand the guest’s plans before agreeing on the itinerary. The guest and operator can then agree on the details before confirming the reservation.

5. What I would carry into another safari build

  • Make choices legible: separate destinations, trip styles and practical planning information.
  • Support comparison: show duration, route, inclusions and pricing context consistently.
  • Offer a direct conversation: make enquiry and WhatsApp actions easy to find.
  • Keep proof specific: use the operator’s genuine business information and verifiable reviews.
  • Validate the finished journey: check phone layouts, image loading and enquiry links before launch, then measure actual visitor behaviour.

The result to inspect here is a structured safari presentation with direct enquiry paths. The useful lesson is how the content and interface work together to help a guest decide what to ask next.
